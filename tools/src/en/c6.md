# 6 | Land navigation | Part II, Lesson 2 — based on the course outline, extended from public sources

## 6.1 | Introduction | web
Orientation is the basis of evasion and extraction: without accurate positioning neither reporting nor link-up is possible. Tools: map, compass, terrain features, and the last resort – the sun and stars.

## 6.2 | Reading a grid reference | web
Military maps use the MGRS/UTM grid: grid lines are 1 km apart (on a 1:50,000 map).
1. Find the bottom-left corner of the square containing the point.
2. **“Along the corridor, then up the stairs”**: read the easting (vertical line) first, then the northing (horizontal line).
3. Refine with tenths (protractor): 6 digits = 100 m, 8 digits = 10 m accuracy.
+ Example: 045-245 → easting 04.5 / northing 24.5 grid units.

## 6.3 | Measuring distance on the map | web
- Scale 1:50,000 → 1 cm on the map = **500 m** on the ground; 1:25,000 → 1 cm = 250 m.
- Straight line: with a ruler / the compass's 1:50,000 scale.
- Winding road: strip of paper, thread, or add up segments.
- On the ground: **pace count** – measure in advance how many paces make 100 m (average 60–70); more uphill, at night, loaded.

## 6.4 | Orienting the map | web
On an oriented map, directions match those on the ground.
1. **With a compass:** lay the compass edge along a north grid line and turn the map with the compass until magnetic north is on the mark (taking declination into account).
2. **By terrain features:** turn the map to the ground using a straight road, railway or distant identifiable point.

## 6.5 | Protractor (coordinate scale) | web
A transparent sheet with degree/mil scales and grid-reference scales.
- Measuring a bearing on the map: centre on the start point, 0° on grid north, read the direction to the target.
- Convert the **grid** azimuth to **magnetic** using the map's declination diagram before using it with a compass.

## 6.6 | Measuring a bearing with the Cammenga 3H compass | web
Parts of the Cammenga lensatic compass: cover with sighting wire, lens eyepiece, floating dial, rotating bezel (click ring), thumb loop, 1:50,000 scale. The 3H model's tritium illumination is visible at night without charging.
**Reading a bearing (compass-to-cheek method):**
1. Open the cover to 90°, the eyepiece to about 45° – the dial must float freely.
2. Thumb through the loop, index finger along the side; support it from below with the other hand.
3. Raise it to eye level, thumb against the cheekbone.
4. Look through the eyepiece: line the target up with the sighting wire.
5. Read the bearing on the dial under the sighting wire.
**Setting a bearing:** the clicking bezel: 1 click = **3°** (or 50 mils); 360° = 120 clicks. It can be set in the dark by counting clicks.
! Near metal objects, vehicles, power lines or weapons the compass errs – step away (at least 1–2 m from a weapon, 10+ m from a vehicle).

## 6.7 | Measuring an azimuth in the field | web
**Measuring:**
1. Choose the target.
2. Sight it along the wire with the compass-to-cheek hold.
3. Wait for the dial to settle, read it.
4. Repeat for accuracy.
**Following a bearing:**
1. Turn the dial (or click the bezel) to the desired azimuth.
2. With the centre hold, turn your body to align the needle with the luminous mark.
3. Pick a feature on that line, walk to it, repeat.
+ At night: the two luminous dots and the needle lining up show the direction.

## 6.8 | Modified resection | web
If you're on a known line (road, river, ridge):
1. Measure the azimuth to a known feature identified on the map.
2. Calculate the back azimuth (±180°).
3. Draw the back azimuth from the feature on the map – where it crosses the known line is where you are.

## 6.9 | Resection | web
If your position is unknown:
1. Orient the map, identify **two (better three)** clearly visible features also on the map, ideally 60–120° apart.
2. Measure each azimuth, calculate the back azimuths (±180°).
3. Draw the lines from the features – the intersection (centre of the triangle) is your position.

## 6.10 | Navigating without tools | web
- **Shadow stick:** mark the tip of a stick's shadow, again 15 minutes later – the first mark is west, the second east.
- **Watch:** in the northern hemisphere point the hour hand at the sun; the bisector between it and 12 (1 in summer time) points south.
- **North Star:** extend the distance between the Big Dipper's two pointer stars five times – north.
! Moss, tree rings and anthills are unreliable direction indicators.
