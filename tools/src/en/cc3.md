# C3 | Resilience and survival in captivity | CHARLIE · based on AFH 10-644

## C3.1 | Stress and the will to survive | web
The greatest enemy in captivity is often not the captor but hopelessness.
- Fear, anger, guilt and sleep problems are normal – normal reactions to an extraordinary situation.
- **Faith and purpose:** family, comrades, coming home, faith or your values – hold on to whatever gives you strength.
- Remember that your country and the allies are working to free you.
+ According to returned prisoners of war, the key to survival was hope and small daily goals.

## C3.2 | Daily routine | web
- **Exercise:** even in a small space: squats, push-ups, stretching – maintenance for body and mind.
- **Mental exercise:** learn, plan, recall memories, build a house in your head, solve problems.
- **Hygiene:** wash as much as you can, keep your space in order.
- **Eating and drinking:** eat what you are given, even if it's bad – you need your strength.
- **Sense of time:** count the days, build a daily schedule.

## C3.3 | Contact with fellow prisoners | web
- Comrades keep each other alive: look out for each other, share your strength.
- The senior prisoner leads the group, and the chain of command works in captivity too.
- Captors try to prevent communication. Vietnam-era POWs, for example, used the publicly known **tap code**: a 5×5 letter grid, tapping first the row, then the column number.
- Don't spread rumours, don't accuse your comrades – division helps the captor.

## C3.4 | Health in captivity | web
- Report illness or injury and ask for treatment – it's your right.
- Keep wounds as clean as you can; watch for signs of infection (redness, swelling, fever, foul smell).
- Water and food may be scarce: pace yourself, rest when you can.
- Watch your comrades' state of mind too: whoever withdraws, stops eating or gives up needs you.
