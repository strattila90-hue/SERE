# 3 | Building shelters | Part I, Lesson 3

## 3.1 | Introduction
At SERE “A” level every soldier is expected to build, on their own and from issued equipment, a shelter that protects against the harmful effects of nature in a friendly environment under field conditions. E-learning alone is not enough – you have to practise.

## 3.2 | Shelter
A shelter can be anything that protects you from environmental hazards. The environment and the situation affect the choice of site and the factors the isolated person must consider.

## 3.3 | Shelter factors
When choosing a site, consider:
- the time and energy needed to build it;
- how long you'll stay (short or longer);
- the weather (temperature, wind, sun, precipitation);
- life forms (human, plant, animal);
- the terrain;
- the time of day;
- the situation (hostile or neutral environment).
+ Maximum protection for minimum energy!

## 3.4 | Considerations – checklist
- Is it needed at all?
- Do I have enough energy to build it?
- What do I have to build it with?
- How long is it for?
- Is it protected enough from the elements?
- Does it need camouflage?
- Are there objective hazards threatening it?
- Do I need heating?
- Is proper ventilation ensured?
! Always allow enough time to choose the shelter site!

## 3.5 | Timing
Don't wait until the last moment: in bad conditions you may be forced to use poor materials and a poor site. Set a deliberate order of priorities between fire, water, food and shelter.
+ Rule of thumb: start building at least 1–2 hours before dark.

## 3.6 | Weather and temperature
Ignoring the weather can have disastrous consequences.
- Valley bottoms are colder – a **“cold-air sink”**: cold air flows down here at night.
- In cold months choose open, sunny ground; in hot months shaded, covered ground.
- In deserts it's scorching by day and freezing at night – you must protect against both.

## 3.7 | Wind
In summer a cool breeze helps and keeps insects away (hilltop, island). Blowing sand, dust or snow irritates skin and eyes and damages equipment; take extra care with fire. In cold you need protection against wind and drifting snow.

## 3.8 | Precipitation
! Don't build in a gully, drainage channel or dry stream bed – sudden floods, mudslides, flash floods.
! In snowy mountains avoid avalanche-prone slopes.

## 3.9 | Life forms
Consider every life form. Humans may be the enemy or another group you must remain unseen by.
- **Insects:** fewer on hills, ridges and breezy spots. Avoid still water (mosquitoes, bees, wasps) and anthills.
- **Animals:** don't build near game trails, dens or watering holes.
- **Dangerous plants:** thorns, vines, poisonous plants. Avoid dead, half-fallen trees and branches – **“widowmakers”** can fall in wind or rain and kill.
- With fire, fallen leaves and dense dry vegetation easily catch.

## 3.10 | Terrain
! Avoid avalanche, rockfall and landslide areas – recognisable as a clear strip of different vegetation from the top of a hill to the bottom.
! Check rock shelters and overhangs before use.

## 3.11 | Requirements and planning principles
A good shelter gives physical and mental well-being for rest, which is vital for good decisions – the longer rescue takes, the more critical this is.
- It should be adequate with little work and energy; don't limit yourself to a predetermined form.
- Use natural shelter (rock overhang, rock niche, cave, crevice, fallen log, root wall, snow wall) and improve it.
- Insulation can be increased with vegetation or a parachute; a heat reflector can be built under a ledge or in front of a cave.

## 3.12 | Defining the shelter's purpose
The type depends on: climate; weather; insects; available materials; length of stay; the enemy's presence, numbers and equipment; the isolated person's physical condition.
! In enemy territory think about traces while building and dismantling: minimal intervention, careful removal.

## 3.13 | Shelter specifications
- Big enough for comfort, but not so big it's a lot of work (in cold, smaller = warmer).
- If you light a fire, ventilate for smoke and carbon monoxide – even if the fire doesn't smoke (fuel tablets, gas, hand sanitiser).
- Fire only outside the shelter; the opening at **90°** to the prevailing wind.
+ Ideal fire-to-shelter distance: approx. **90–100 cm**.
! Don't build a fire near aircraft or vehicle wreckage (spilled fuel). Move your materials a safe distance away instead.

## 3.14 | Protection from rain and snow
With porous (non-waterproof) material use the **“pitch and tautness”** principle: a pitch of **45–60°**, with the material taut and wrinkle-free. Don't touch the material while water runs over it – it will drip through there.

## 3.15 | Materials, site, frame
**Collecting materials:** frame, covering, bed/insulation, fasteners – all at hand before you start.
1. Remove all rough edges and stubs from the poles of a wooden frame.
2. For a natural shelter leave branches on the outer side of the tree – they're good supports.
3. For frame and cover: bark, cut branches, palm leaves, grass, woven vegetation.
4. With a parachute, remove the lines and cut to size.
**Preparing the site:** clear rocks and twigs from the bed, cut back overhanging vegetation.
**Frame:** it must carry snow, rain and gusts. A **60°** pitch is optimal. With a parachute, tension the centre seam, work from back to front, pull at 90° to the folds. With natural covering use the **shingle method**: from the bottom up, overlapping – you shouldn't be able to see through from inside.

## 3.16 | Types of shelter
**By material:** natural, man-made, combined.
**By camouflage:** conspicuous or concealed.
**By construction:** existing, hasty, improvised.
Climate zones: hot (equatorial, tropical, desert, savanna, monsoon), temperate (oceanic, Mediterranean, continental), cold (subarctic), and mountain.

## 3.17 | Requirements for a concealed shelter
- Close to water, food, fuel, signal or extraction site.
- Safe, protects from environmental hazards; enough material nearby.
- Big enough to sit, lie and store gear; level floor.
- Hard to approach, in the least obvious place.
- Several approach and escape routes; can be left immediately.
- Natural camouflage (against aerial observation too); irregular shape, blending colour.
- Easy to dismantle (quick-release knots).
**Conspicuous (uncamouflaged) shelter:** easy to approach, in an obvious place, marked with bright colour and light.

## 3.18 | Existing, hasty, improvised
- **Existing:** ledges, fallen trunks, caves, tree wells; in towns, buildings. If one exists, use it – only build new if necessary.
- **Hasty:** quick, minimal effort: life raft, vehicle or aircraft parts, parachute.
- **Improvised:** quick to build and dismantle (lean-to, diamond, A-frame, sunshade, teepee) – only if you don't need to flee immediately.

## 3.19 | Temperate-zone shelters
1. **Cave** – easy to heat, cold without fire; obvious place marked on maps; no escape route; predators.
2. **Rock niche, overhang** – shaded, draughty, protects from precipitation; hard to reach, no escape route.
3. **Sod shelter** – sod blocks about 5 cm thick on a strong frame; can be heated, cool in summer, water- and insect-proof, fireproof. Needs a spade; time-, material- and energy-intensive – long-term only.
4. **Debris (A-frame) shelter** – in deciduous forest, camouflages excellently; lots of material and time; insects; risky to heat with fire.
5. **Fallen tree, improved** – check the tree's stability; camouflages well, limited protection from wind and rain.

## 3.20 | Tarp-based shelters
A soldier's own tarp-type gear: issue tent sheet, poncho, parachute, 2–3×3 m tarp, emergency blanket, bin bag.
6. **Lean-to** – the quickest, keeps rain off well, can't hold snow; windproof from one side; fire warms a large surface. At 60°+ even non-waterproof material sheds water.
7. **Modified lean-to with porch** – from an A-frame by raising one side; more cordage; take extra care with fire distance.
8. **Lean-to with ground sheet** – the folded-back part insulates against ground moisture.
9. **Diamond** – one anchor point is enough; windproof over 180°; heats well.
10. **A-frame** – excellent rain protection, windproof from two sides, hard to heat.
11. **Modified A-frame** – closed back, windproof from several sides.
12. **Raised A-frame** – hot climates (jungle); airy, no wind protection.
13. **Teepee (parachute)** – excellent against wind, rain, cold and insects, fire possible inside; too big for a concealed shelter, but good for signalling at night.
14. **Teepee (tent sheets) – “pup tent”** – two buttoned issue tent sheets; the centre pole must be stable; leaks if not taut.
15. **Burrow / bivvy** – dry insulation in a waterproof cover, “do as the animals do”; warmed by body heat.
16. **Vehicle wreckage** – wing, tail or fuselage for shade; inside overheats in heat and cools in frost; fire danger!
17. **Buildings, ruins** – abandoned buildings, sheds, garages, culverts; don't hide in the only obvious spot; change locations if possible.

## 3.21 | Hot-zone shelters
**Tropics, jungle:** on hills, high ground, away from swamps; few places to build directly on the ground.
18. **Raised platform bed** – for wet, swampy ground; time-, tool- and material-intensive.
19. **Hammock** – tarp or parachute between two load-bearing points; quick to strike, leaves few traces; hard to insulate in cold.
**Desert:** build in the early morning, evening or at night. Shade of rocks and dunes; the material 30–40 cm above you.
20. **Improvised sunshade** – 2–3 layers with a 30–40 cm air gap between; the bed about 40 cm above or below the surface.

## 3.22 | Snow and ice – principles
In a small, well-built snow shelter the temperature rarely drops below –10 °C. Body heat raises it by about 4 °C, one candle by another 4 °C or so.
+ If you can't see your breath, it's too warm inside – cool it, or it will melt and drip.
- Three levels: fire/vent at the top, sleeping platform in the middle, cold sink at the bottom.
- Scrape off the glazed (iced) inner layer.
- Keep the old shelter until the new one is ready.
! Physical work makes your body produce up to 1500 W of heat – take off layers and open zips before building. **Sweat is deadly in the cold!** Work slowly, at an even pace.

## 3.23 | Entrance, ventilation, hygiene
- Opening at 90° to the prevailing wind; close the entrance with a snow block or pack.
- Take the shovel inside at night – if snow buries you, you can dig out.
! Candles, burners and oil lamps produce carbon monoxide: have vents in the roof and by the door.
- Hygiene: stay inside if possible; a separate tunnel as a toilet; bottles, snow blocks.

## 3.24 | Barren and wooded areas
**Barren:** seashore, ice cap, above the treeline; little material. Build on the lee side of wind-blown ridges. Avoid unstable ice.
21. **Snow cave** – needs a shovel; quick, excellent wind protection; ventilation; avalanche risk.
22. **Quinzee** (packed snow mound) – time-consuming, lots of energy; clothes get soaked – dry them straight away.
23. **Igloo** – needs a cutting tool and firm snow; lots of time and precision.
**Wooded:** plenty of material; not on riverbanks (floods, mudflows).
24. **Tree well** – the hollow under a spruce's low branches; dig from the lee side; don't knock snow off the branches.
25. **Snow trench** – at least 1–1.5 m of snow; a digging tool; quick and low-effort with a tarp; covered with boughs and snow it insulates and camouflages.

## 3.25 | Maintenance and improvements
For longer use maintain it: a taut tarp protects better; a door against wind and insects; heat reflector, porch, work area, separate storage for gear and fuel.

## 3.26 | Sleep system – sleeping bag
A sleeping bag only gives maximum warmth when clean, dry and fluffed. Turn it inside out, shake it, dry it in the sun or at a safe distance from the fire.
! Don't breathe into the bag (keep nose and mouth outside) – breath makes it damp. Don't keep a water bottle in it.
+ Before getting in: light exercise (without sweating), food and a hot drink.
**Improvised sleeping bag:** parachute, sheet or blanket folded lengthwise, sewn or tied along the bottom and side, 15–25 cm longer than your height. With a double layer fill the gap with dry grass, straw, cardboard, newspaper, foam.

## 3.27 | Ground pad – ground insulation
You need insulation toward the ground too: natural (dry leaves, pine boughs, reed) or man-made (foam, inflatable mat). The air layer insulates – don't let it compress.
! Even in a winter bag rated –25 °C you'll be cold at 0 °C without ground insulation. Cooling can start below 15 °C!
- **R-value:** a mat's insulating ability – the higher, the better. About 1.0 (minimal) to 5+ (excellent, winter).
- Every extra kilo counts on a march.
**Man-made:** sleeping bag, blanket, poncho, tent sheet, 3×3 m tarp, parachute, emergency blanket, bin bag, foam and inflatable mats.
**Natural:** dry grass, leaf litter, reed bundles, straw bales, pine boughs, framed bed lined with natural material.
**Accessories:** tent pegs, wooden stakes, 4–5 m of 5–6 mm rope, 5–6 m paracord.

## 3.28 | Knots
! Some knots are also used in climbing and rope work, but different rules apply there. Using them incorrectly is dangerous and can kill!
- **Overhand knot** – fastening, grip knot, rope end. + Quick, basis of other knots. − Hard to untie, can slip. ⏱ 10 s.
- **Slip knot** – running knot. Fastening. + Quick to tie and untie. − Less secure. ⏱ 15 s.
- **Overhand loop** – a fixed loop along or at the end of the rope; grip, hanging point, tensioning eye. + Can be tied when neither end is free. − Almost impossible to untie after loading. ⏱ 15 s.
- **Half hitch** – tying bundles and packages. + Easy to untie. − Insecure. ⏱ 20 s.
