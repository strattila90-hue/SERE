# 2 | Fire making | Part I, Lesson 2 – Lighting fires with lighters, matches and improvised means

## 2.1 | Why fire matters
Being able to make fire is one of the most important survival skills. Fire can be used:
- to warm yourself, heat things up and dry clothes;
- as a light source and for signalling (light, flame, smoke);
- for making tools, cooking and baking;
- for purifying water (boiling, charcoal);
- to scare off wild animals and keep insects away (smoke);
- to lift morale, and charcoal for camouflage.
+ When you use fire to warm up, your body burns fewer calories to keep its temperature – you need less food.
! In hostile territory the light, smoke and smell of a fire can easily give away your position. Knowing the methods is not enough – you need skill gained through practice.

## 2.2 | Considerations
- Avoid big fires: a small fire needs less fuel, is easier to control and its heat is more concentrated.
- Never leave a fire unattended unless you have safely banked or put it out.
+ Banking: rake cold ash and dry soil over the fire, leaving enough air at the top for the fuel to smoulder. It can be revived later.

## 2.3 | Safety
Distinguish between an emergency and peacetime training bound by rules. Always keep in mind the protection of human life, animals, nature, property and facilities.
! Never underestimate the power of fire – it can get out of control in moments.
! In cold weather burns are more common than cold injuries. Use protective gloves, a multitool, an insulated handle.
! Exhaustion and carelessness can burn equipment too (radio, boots, poncho) – fatal in a survival situation.
! In enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces smoke (carbon monoxide) poisoning can occur – ensure ventilation.
- In group or pair training, always assign a fire guard.
- Keep extinguishing means (water, sand, spade, shovel) at hand.

## 2.4 | Elements of fire
The three basic elements of a successful fire are the **fire triangle**:
1. fuel;
2. heat;
3. oxygen.
Limiting the fuel only allows a small fire. If the fire is not fed properly, it can be too big or too small. Freshly cut green wood ignites poorly – the fire must already be burning well to use it.
+ Taking away any element (covering, water, removing fuel) puts the fire out – think this way when extinguishing.

## 2.5 | The three levels of fuel
1. **Tinder** – easily ignited material;
2. **Kindling**;
3. **Fuel** – firewood.
> When you think you've got enough tinder, kindling and firewood, triple it – and then maybe it'll be enough.

## 2.6 | Tinder
Any small material with a low ignition point that catches from minimal heat (e.g. a spark). Arrange it so that air can get between the hair-thin, bone-dry fibres.
+ Dry tinder is vital for the isolated person – always keep some at hand in a waterproof pouch.
- It can be collected in two or three stages: keep collecting and drying it as you move (e.g. in your pocket, close to your body).

## 2.7 | Natural tinder
- shredded bark of certain trees and shrubs: cedar, palm fibre, **birch bark** (burns even when wet);
- the lining of bird or rodent nests;
- charred rotten wood (punk wood);
- dry tinder fungus;
- the fluff of milkweed, thistle, clematis, reed;
- crushed fibres of dead plants (straw, hay);
- fine, dry wood shavings;
- scrapings of resinous wood (fatwood);
- dried, powdered conifer resin (pitch).

## 2.8 | Artificial tinder
- cotton wool and products: tampons, cotton pads;
- char cloth, cotton balls, lint;
- plastic shavings (e.g. an MRE spoon);
- petroleum fuels (kerosene, petrol);
- steel wool (also lights from battery terminals);
- paper, foam;
- alcohol-based hand sanitiser;
- gunpowder;
- fire-starter tablets.
! Petrol vapour is explosive – use only a small amount, pour it on cold tinder before lighting.

## 2.9 | Kindling
The next level of fuel. It has a higher ignition point and must be placed so it catches when the tinder's flame reaches it. Its job is to raise the burning temperature to where larger fuel can feed the fire.
**Natural:** dead, dry small twigs; thinly split pieces of wood, bamboo or reed; shavings carved from the inside of larger branches; cones, needles; the dead lower branches of conifers.
! Always split bamboo and elder – whole, they can explode because of the trapped air.
**Artificial:** some plastics (MRE spoon); wood soaked in wax, oil, insect repellent; vaseline-soaked gauze from the first aid kit.
+ Split wood burns more easily because it's drier inside; sharp edges catch more easily. Sorted fuel catches more easily.

## 2.10 | Fuel
Firewood doesn't need to be completely dry – a steadily burning fire dries it. Still, protect it from moisture because of smoke.
! Never pour flammable liquid onto a burning fire – even a smouldering fire can flare explosively.
- Dry split hardwood (oak, hickory, ash): little smoke, lots of heat, but harder to cut.
- Pine and conifers: burn fast, smoke a lot.
- Rotten wood: little heat, mostly smoulders and smokes.
- Standing or leaning dead trees: usually dry inside even after rain.
**Recommended fuel:** standing dead trees and dry branches that snap; the inside of fallen trees; as a last resort green wood split fine and mixed with dry; in treeless areas twisted bundles of dry grass, cactus, dry peat moss, dry animal dung, animal fat, oil-soaked sand.

## 2.11 | Preparing to make fire
Take enough time to prepare: collect every level of fuel and prepare the fire starter **before** lighting.
+ Success = preparation + practice + patience.

## 2.12 | The fire site
Clear the fire site carefully and keep a safe distance from natural (leaf litter, dry grass) and man-made (pack, tent) flammables – horizontally and **vertically** too (washing line, low branches).
! Wind strength and direction can change – embers can burn the shelter tarp or start a fire.
- To contain the fire: metal stove, a pit dug in the ground, a ring of stones.
! Don't dig a fire pit near pine trees: resinous roots act like wicks and can start a forest fire days later.
! Wet, porous or hollow stones can explode from the heat – don't use riverbank stones.
- In snow, dig down to the ground – the pit must be wide and airy enough, or melting snow or lack of oxygen will put it out.
- On wet, marshy ground you need a platform (stones, green logs).

## 2.13 | Get the most heat
Use a **heat reflector** built of stone, wood or other material (emergency blanket, metal sheet) to direct heat into the shelter. Logs/stones around the fire support a cooking pot and also act as a windbreak.

## 2.14 | Rationing fuel
Stack and sort it by size (tinder, kindling, firewood).
> Rule of thumb: collect three times as much tinder and kindling as seems necessary.
+ For a long stay, ration responsibly and protect the environment – don't waste!

## 2.15 | Building a fire platform
You need a base if the fire would melt through deep snow, or the ground is wet or marshy. It can be made of green logs, metal or non-flammable material.
! On humus or peat soil you always need a platform: a smouldering peat fire is almost impossible to put out and can burn for years.

## 2.16 | Fire starters
Should be quick and easy to use, even with gloves: matches (storm matches), lighter, storm lighter, magnesium block, ferro rod, flint striker.
+ Always carry at least 2–3 independent fire starters stored in separate places (“two is one, one is none”).

## 2.17 | Arranging the kindling
Arrange a small amount of kindling in a low teepee, close enough for the flames to jump across. Leave a small opening for lighting and airflow. Paper is the easiest tinder.

## 2.18 | Using a feather stick
You can save matches with a feather stick or an airy bundle of thin dry twigs. Shield the flame from wind; watch the wind direction so the flame doesn't catch your hair, clothes or gear. Place the tinder under the kindling on the windward side.

## 2.19 | Small sticks to support the kindling
Before or after lighting, carefully add small sticks to the kindling, then gradually bigger ones. A forearm-thick piece of wood as a support stops the fire from being smothered and keeps it ventilated.

## 2.20 | Ways to light a fire
> Teaching and using these is part of SERE “B” and “C” training – here they appear as knowledge only.
**Sunlight (magnification)** – needs strong, clear sun and good tinder: magnifying glass (also on a compass), glasses, Fresnel lens, magnifying mirror, the reflector of a torch, a polished drinks-can bottom, a crystal-clear bottle/glass/bag of water.
**Electricity** – heat of a short circuit: thin wire, chewing-gum foil, steel wool + battery.
! Electrical methods carry a risk of accidents and explosion and can cause a fatal electric shock!
**Friction** – bow drill, hand drill, fire plough, bamboo fire saw, fire roll.
**Spark** – ferro rod; flint and steel (knife, file).

## 2.21 | Other methods
**Mini stove (solid-fuel stove):** compact folding steel stove with solid fuel tablets. One tablet is 10–15 g and burns about 8–10 minutes – for heating food without open fire.
**Plastic items:** stick the handle of a plastic spoon upright in the ground and light the tip – it burns about 10 minutes, enough to dry and light tinder. A candle saves matches the same way.

## 2.22 | Fire lays
Most fires are built for a specific need (warmth, light, boiling water, cooking). Names vary in the literature.
- **Teepee:** good light, concentrated heat above the top, ideal for boiling water. Tinder in the middle, kindling leaning on a stake around it, opening on the windward side. Light it with your back to the wind.
- **Log cabin:** even, long-burning bed of embers, for cooking.
- **Long fire (Finnish fire):** two logs on top of each other, burns all night, for warmth.
- **Upside-down (top-lit) fire:** needs little feeding.
- **Star (shepherd's) fire:** burns economically as you push the logs inward.
- **Lean-to fire:** sheltered from wind, kindling leaning.
- **Dakota fire hole:** two connected pits – little light and smoke, burns well in wind, good for concealment.
